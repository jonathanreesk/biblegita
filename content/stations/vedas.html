The Vedas and the Upanishads

These are the oldest Hindu texts, and Hindus rank them highest of all. They are not a story. The Vedas are hymns and ritual instructions, and the Upanishads are philosophy. Here is what they teach, how they answer the five questions of a fair test, and what the Bible says instead.

The Vedas: what they are about

The Vedas are not a story. They are about a thousand hymns and sets of ritual instructions, composed for priests to use in worship. Their center is sacrifice (yajna): offering gifts, usually into a fire, to the gods so that the world keeps running and the people are blessed.

  • HymnsRig VedaAbout a thousand hymns to gods such as Indra, Agni and Varuna. The oldest.
  • RitualYajur VedaThe words and actions priests use in the sacrifice.
  • ChantSama VedaMelodies for chanting, mostly from Rig Veda verses.
  • Everyday prayersAtharva VedaFor health, protection, family and harvest.
Theme of the VedasThe world is held in an order (Rta). The gods uphold it, and people keep it by sacrifice.
  1. The problemBeing out of step with the gods and the order of the world.
  2. The way outThe right ritual, done correctly.
  3. The goalBlessing in this life, such as rain, cattle, children and long life, and a place in heaven.
  • One truth, many names. The Rig Veda says "Truth is one, but the wise speak of it in many ways" (Rig Veda 1.164.46).
  • Unsure how the world began. Its creation hymn (Rig Veda 10.129) asks who really knows how the world began, and wonders whether even the one who watches from the highest heaven knows.
  • No story of how the world went wrong. Some hymns ask the god Varuna, the guardian of order, to forgive wrongdoing. But they never explain how wrongdoing entered the world.
  • Karma and rebirth came later. The early Vedas do not yet teach karma as a moral law, repeated rebirth or moksha. Most scholars find these ideas first appear in the Upanishads, though Hindu tradition sees them as already implied in the Vedas.

The Upanishads: what they are about

The Upanishads are the last layer of the Vedas. The name means "sitting near" a teacher. About thirteen are counted as the main ones, composed from roughly 800 to 500 BCE. Most are conversations in which a student asks the biggest questions: who am I, what is ultimate reality, and what happens when we die?

The main Upanishads at a glance
  • IshaEverything belongs to God, so enjoy the world without clinging to it.
  • KenaAsks what makes us able to think, see and hear. Its answer: Brahman.
  • KathaA boy, Nachiketa, asks the god of death what happens after we die.
  • PrashnaSix students ask a teacher where life comes from and what keeps it going.
  • MundakaSays rituals cannot save you. Only knowing Brahman can.
  • MandukyaThe shortest one. Explains the sacred sound Om through waking, dreaming, deep sleep and a fourth state beyond them.
  • TaittiriyaPictures the self as five layers, from the body on the outside to pure joy at the center.
  • AitareyaTells how the world was made, and says awareness itself is Brahman.
  • ChandogyaKnown for the teaching "That you are": your true self is Brahman.
  • BrihadaranyakaThe longest one. A sage, Yajnavalkya, debates others and teaches "I am Brahman".
  • KaushitakiDescribes where the soul goes after death, and calls the breath of life Brahman.
  • ShvetashvataraUnusual among them: it calls for loving devotion to a personal Lord, Shiva.

Most are named after the teacher or school that kept them. Shankara wrote commentaries on the first ten, which is why they are treated as the core.

Theme of the UpanishadsBrahman, the one ultimate reality, and Atman, your true self, are the same.
  1. The problemIgnorance (avidya): not knowing who you really are.
  2. The way outKnowledge, usually learned from a teacher.
  3. The goalMoksha: release from the cycle of rebirth.
  • Key line"Tat tvam asi", "That you are"A father teaches his son that his true self is Brahman (Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7).
  • Key line"Aham brahmasmi", "I am Brahman"(Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 1.4.10)
  • StoryNachiketa and DeathA boy asks Yama, the lord of death, what happens after we die (Katha Upanishad 1.1).
  • StoryYajnavalkya and MaitreyiA sage teaches his wife about the self (Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 2.4).
  • Karma and rebirth first appear clearly here. What you do shapes what you become, and the next life follows from it. Knowledge is what sets you free.
  • Ritual is not enough. The Upanishads treat the Vedic sacrifices as a lesser path. One compares them to an unsteady boat that cannot carry you across (Mundaka Upanishad 1.2.7).
  • Two pictures of God. Some passages describe an impersonal Brahman without qualities. Others speak of a personal Lord. This is why Advaita, Vishishtadvaita and Dvaita all claim the Upanishads as their source.

Expect this reply

  • "Every way of knowing starts somewhere." Hindu thinkers say tradition is a valid source of knowledge, like sight or logic, and that the Vedas are proven in the experience of those who practise them.

Your strongest answer

Agree that every view has starting points. The difference is whether a starting point can be checked from outside. Experience inside a tradition cannot test the tradition. The Bible rests on public events, above all the resurrection of Jesus (1 Corinthians 15:3-8), and invites anyone to examine them.

What they ask, and what the Bible announces

The Vedas and Upanishads asks

How do we keep the gods and the world in order, and who am I really? They answer with ritual and with knowledge, and never deal with sin.

The Bible announces

God has come to redeem rebels and make them His children: "as many as received him, to them he gave the right to become God's children" (John 1:12).

Three of these problems come back later as full questions: love in "Monism and the personal God", the endless chain in "Karma and the endless chain", and checking the texts in "How the texts are built".

Christ above all

The Vedas look for the right words and the right ritual to reach the gods. The Upanishads look for knowledge to set a person free. The Bible says God did not leave us searching: He "has at the end of these days spoken to us by his Son" (Hebrews 1:1-2).
