The two stories

The Vedas and the Upanishads

These are the oldest Hindu texts, and Hindus rank them highest of all. They are not a story. The Vedas are hymns and ritual instructions, and the Upanishads are philosophy. Here is what they teach, where they go wrong, and what the Bible says instead.

The Vedas: what they are about

The Vedas are not a story. They are about a thousand hymns and sets of ritual instructions, composed for priests to use in worship. Their center is sacrifice (yajna): offering gifts, usually into a fire, to the gods so that the world keeps running and the people are blessed.

The four Vedas
  • Rig Veda About a thousand hymns praising gods such as Indra, Agni and Varuna. The oldest.
  • Yajur Veda The words and actions priests use in the sacrifice.
  • Sama Veda Melodies for chanting, taken mostly from Rig Veda verses.
  • Atharva Veda Prayers for everyday needs: health, protection, family and harvest.
Theme and framework
  • Theme The world is held in an order (Rta). The gods uphold it, and people keep it by sacrifice.
  • The problem Being out of step with the gods and the order of the world.
  • The way out The right ritual, done correctly.
  • The goal Blessing in this life, such as rain, cattle, children and long life, and a place in heaven.
  • Many gods, one truth? The Rig Veda says "Truth is one, but the wise speak of it in many ways" (Rig Veda 1.164.46).
  • Honest doubt. Its creation hymn (Rig Veda 10.129) asks who really knows how the world began, and wonders whether even the one who watches from the highest heaven knows.
  • Wrongdoing. Some hymns ask the god Varuna, the guardian of order, to forgive wrongdoing. But the Vedas have no single story of how the world went wrong.
  • Not yet in the early Vedas. Most scholars find that the full ideas of karma as a moral law, repeated rebirth and moksha appear later, in the Upanishads. Hindu tradition sees them as already implied earlier.

The Upanishads: what they are about

The Upanishads are the last layer of the Vedas. The name means "sitting near" a teacher. About thirteen are counted as the main ones, composed from roughly 800 to 500 BCE. Most are conversations in which a student asks the biggest questions: who am I, what is ultimate reality, and what happens when we die?

Theme and framework
  • Theme Brahman, the one ultimate reality, and Atman, your true self, are the same.
  • The problem Ignorance (avidya): not knowing who you really are.
  • The way out Knowledge, usually learned from a teacher.
  • The goal Moksha: release from the cycle of rebirth.
Key lines and stories
  • "Tat tvam asi", "That you are" A father teaches his son that his true self is Brahman (Chandogya Upanishad).
  • "Aham brahmasmi", "I am Brahman" (Brihadaranyaka Upanishad).
  • Nachiketa and Death A boy asks Yama, the lord of death, what happens after we die (Katha Upanishad).
  • Yajnavalkya and Maitreyi A sage teaches his wife about the self (Brihadaranyaka Upanishad).
  • Karma and rebirth first appear clearly here. What you do shapes what you become, and the next life follows from it. Knowledge is what sets you free.
  • Ritual is not enough. The Upanishads treat the Vedic sacrifices as a lesser path. One compares them to an unsteady boat that cannot carry you across.
  • Not one voice. Some passages describe an impersonal Brahman without qualities. Others speak of a personal Lord. This is why Advaita, Vishishtadvaita and Dvaita all claim the Upanishads as their source.

Where they go wrong

Ask the Vedas and Upanishads five simple questions, and compare their answers with the Bible's. They fail on every one.

Who vouches for them?
The Vedas and Upanishads

No one outside themThey are said to be apaurusheya, made by no one: eternal sounds that seers "heard". There is no author, no date and no witness. The only proof offered for the claim is the claim itself, which is circular reasoning.

The Bible

Named writers and witnessesMoses, David, Isaiah, Luke, John, Peter and Paul wrote in real places and times. Luke says he "traced the course of all things accurately from the first" (Luke 1:1-4), and Peter says, "we were eyewitnesses of his majesty" (2 Peter 1:16).

Do they agree with themselves?
The Vedas and Upanishads

NoThe early hymns praise Indra, Agni and Varuna as highest, each in turn. Creation is told three different ways, and one hymn admits that even the highest one may not know how the world began (Rig Veda 10.129). The Upanishads then call the Vedic sacrifices an unsteady boat, and they speak of God both as an impersonal Brahman and as a personal Lord. Three Hindu schools read them in three opposite ways.

The Bible

One God, one storyFrom the first page to the last there is one God, who creates, speaks, acts and saves: "I am God, and there is no other" (Isaiah 45:22).

What do they do with wrongdoing?
The Vedas and Upanishads

Ritual, or knowledgeThe Vedas keep the gods pleased with sacrifice, and a few hymns ask Varuna to forgive, but they never say how the world went wrong or how guilt is taken away. The Upanishads say the real problem is not sin at all, but ignorance of who you are.

The Bible

Sin, and a sacrifice that ends all sacrificesSin is rebellion against a holy God. The animal sacrifices of the Old Testament pointed forward to one sacrifice: Jesus, who offered Himself "once for all" (Hebrews 10:10). "The blood of Jesus Christ, his Son, cleanses us from all sin" (1 John 1:7).

Is there a rescuer?
The Vedas and Upanishads

NoIn the Vedas, the priest and the ritual do the work. In the Upanishads, you free yourself through knowledge, usually over many lives.

The Bible

Yes, God Himself"The Son of Man came to seek and to save that which was lost" (Luke 19:10).

Where does it end?
The Vedas and Upanishads

The person disappearsThe goal of the Upanishads is for the self to merge into Brahman, where there is no "you" and no "God" to love. Meanwhile the ages keep turning.

The Bible

The person is raised and loved foreverGod lives with His people, and "he will wipe away every tear from their eyes" (Revelation 21:3-4).

Expect this reply

  • "Every way of knowing starts somewhere." Hindu thinkers say tradition is a valid source of knowledge, like sight or logic, and that the Vedas are proven in the experience of those who practise them.

Your strongest answer

Agree that every view has starting points. The difference is whether a starting point can be checked from outside. Experience inside a tradition cannot test the tradition. The Bible rests on public events, above all the resurrection of Jesus, and invites anyone to examine them.

What they ask, and what the Bible announces

The Vedas and Upanishads asks

How do we keep the gods and the world in order, and who am I really? They answer with ritual and with knowledge, and leave sin unpaid.

The Bible announces

God has come to rescue rebels and make them His children: "as many as received him, to them he gave the right to become God's children" (John 1:12).

Three of these problems come back later as full questions: love in "Monism and the personal God", the endless chain in "Karma and the endless chain", and checking the texts in "How the texts are built".

Christ above all

The Vedas look for the right words and the right ritual to reach the gods. The Upanishads look for knowledge to set a person free. The Bible says God did not leave us searching: He "has at the end of these days spoken to us by his Son" (Hebrews 1:1-2).
