Can the texts be trusted?

How the texts were passed down

Knowing who wrote a text is the first half. The second is how it reached us. The Vedas and Upanishads (Shruti, "what is heard") were kept by memory. The Mahabharata, the Ramayana and the Puranas (Smriti, "what is remembered") grew through performance and retelling. The Bible was written down, copied by hand and compared.

How a text is handed to the next generation decides how much you can trust what reaches you. Here are three very different ways it was done:

How was it handed on?
The Vedas and Upanishads

Memorized word for wordTrained reciters taught each generation, using strict rules of sound and rhythm.

The epics and Puranas

Performed and retoldStorytellers sang and acted the Mahabharata, the Ramayana and the Puranas in courts, temples and villages, shaping them for each audience. For centuries they lived mainly in the telling, not on a page.

The Bible

Written, copied and comparedNamed authors wrote it down. It was copied by hand in Hebrew (the Masoretic text), in Greek translation (the Septuagint), and in thousands of Greek New Testament copies.

Could the story change?
The Vedas and Upanishads

Almost neverThe sound itself was guarded.

The epics and Puranas

Yes, and it was welcome toEach region and teller added or reshaped scenes, so many versions grew side by side. The Mahabharata split into northern and southern versions, both treated as valid.

The Bible

Copyists made small slipsBut copies were compared with each other, so the differences can be found. The church recognized a closed list of writings already in use.

Can you check the original?
The Vedas and Upanishads

Only against other recitersThere is no outside witness to compare with.

The epics and Puranas

No single original survivesIn the 20th century, scholars rebuilt a core text of the Mahabharata (in Pune) and the Ramayana (in Baroda) from many manuscripts. For the Puranas there is no agreed text at all.

The Bible

YesThousands of copies from different places, some within a few centuries of the events, can be laid side by side to recover the original wording.

What happened to the other Hindu texts

  • The UpanishadsThe list kept growingAbout thirteen main ones, 108 in a traditional list, and over two hundred that carry the name. Some were written a thousand years after the first, one (the "Allah Upanishad") in Akbar's time, in the 1500s.
  • The RamayanaMany RamayanasValmiki's northern and southern versions do not share about a third of their verses, the first and last books were probably added later, and there are hundreds of retellings, including Jain ones where Lakshmana, not Rama, kills Ravana.
  • The PuranasNo fixed textEven the lists of the eighteen "major" Puranas disagree. The Bhavishya Purana "foretells" Queen Victoria in passages written in the 1800s.
  • The law booksEdited over timeThe manuscripts of the Manusmriti differ, and its own commentators argued over which verses were genuine.

Apart from the guarded sound of the Vedas, Hindu tradition did not try to recover what the first author said. Texts were expected to grow. The Bible's whole history of copying rests on the opposite question: what exactly was first written?

Inerrancy and the manuscripts

Christians hold that the Bible, as God gave it, is true and without error in all that it affirms. This is called . It rests on what Scripture says about itself: it is "breathed out by God" (2 Timothy 3:16), written by people who "spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit" (2 Peter 1:21), and Jesus prayed, "Your word is truth" (John 17:17). The claim is about the original writings. So a fair question follows: how do we know the copies we hold today match them?

Codex Sinaiticus

One of the best answers sits in the British Library. Codex Sinaiticus is a Greek Bible copied by hand around AD 350, about 300 years after the apostles wrote, and it contains the complete New Testament along with much of the Old. It was found at St Catherine's Monastery on Mount Sinai, where it takes its name, and the surviving pages are now held in four countries and can be read page by page online at codexsinaiticus.org ↗.

Its value is that it can be compared. Set Sinaiticus beside Codex Vaticanus (about the same age) and the thousands of other Greek copies from different places and centuries, and the same text appears. The differences are mostly spelling and word order. Scribes also corrected it, and those corrections can still be seen on the pages.

Why it matters

Inerrancy is not a claim that every copyist was perfect. It is a claim about what God inspired, and hundreds of independent copies let us check how closely we hold it. The few longer passages in question are footnoted in good Bibles, and no teaching of the faith depends on them.
