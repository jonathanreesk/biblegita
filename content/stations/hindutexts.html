The two stories

The Hindu texts, and where they fit

The Gita is one small part of a very large body of Hindu texts. So where do the others, the Vedas and the Upanishads, sit? Are they inside the Mahabharata, or separate? And how do they fit together into one Hindu view of the world?

The short answer

The Vedas and the Upanishads are not inside the Mahabharata. They are older and separate. Scholars date the Vedas to about 1500 to 1000 BCE and the Upanishads to about 800 to 500 BCE, centuries before the Mahabharata took shape (about 400 BCE to 400 CE). The epic refers to Vedic ideas, but the Vedas and Upanishads stand on their own. The Gita is the bridge: it sits in the epic, but Hindus treat it as if it were an Upanishad.

Two layers of texts

Hindu tradition sorts its texts into two layers. The first is Shruti, "what was heard", the oldest and most respected. The second is Smriti, "what was remembered", which came later.

Shruti: the Vedas (older, outside the epic)

There are four Vedas: Rig, Yajur, Sama and Atharva. Each is built in layers. Shruti is the first and most respected layer.

  • Hymns Prayers and songs to gods such as Indra and Agni. The Rig Veda is the oldest.
  • Rituals Instructions for the sacrifices and ceremonies, which keep the order of the world (called Rta).
  • Forest teachings Reflections for those who stepped back from daily life.
  • Upanishads The last layer of the Vedas. They ask: who am I, and what is ultimate reality? This is where Brahman and Atman are explained. A famous line is "Tat tvam asi", "That you are": the true self is one with Brahman.
Smriti: later texts (the epic is here)

Teaching for everyday life, told through stories. The epics are called itihasa, "so it was": narrative that Hindus treat as history-like. They are later and carry less authority than the Vedas.

  • The Mahabharata The great epic, with the Gita inside it.
  • The Ramayana The second great epic, the story of Rama.
  • The Puranas Stories of the gods, the creation of the world and the ages of time.
  • Law books Teaching on duty and how to live in society.

Where the Gita fits

The Gita is part of the Mahabharata, so strictly it belongs to the later layer. Yet the great Hindu schools treat it as one of three foundations for understanding reality, alongside the Upanishads and a short summary text. Because of this some call the Gita an Upanishad. The Mahabharata even calls itself "the fifth Veda". So the Gita connects the old Vedic teaching to the epic and to the devotion of ordinary Hindus.

How it fits the whole Hindu view

Many Hindus see the texts as three roads that lead to the same goal, which is release from the cycle of rebirth (moksha):

The Gita is unusual because it brings all three together in one conversation, which is one reason it is so loved.

The gods

Hindu texts speak about many gods, and the picture changes across the layers.

  • In the Vedas the main gods are forces of nature and order, such as Indra (storm and king of gods), Agni (fire), Varuna (cosmic order) and Surya (sun).
  • In the later texts three gods stand out: Brahma the creator, Vishnu the preserver and Shiva the destroyer and renewer. Many Hindus worship one of them above the others.
  • Avatars. An avatar is a god coming down to earth in a form. Vishnu is said to have come many times, in ten classic forms. The best known are Rama and Krishna.
  • One or many? Many Hindus say the many gods are different faces of the one reality, Brahman. Others hold that one Lord, such as Vishnu or Shiva, is supreme. There is no single Hindu answer.

Good and bad in the gods

In many of the stories the gods show anger, jealousy, pride and desire, as people do. Most Hindus do not read this as a problem. They read the stories as lessons: take what is good, and leave the rest. It is the same way many read the Mahabharata, where no one is fully good and no one fully bad.

The Hindu reading

The gods and the heroes are mixed. Pick up the good, ignore the bad, and learn from what happens when people do each.

What the Bible says of God

God is good all the way through: "God is light, and in him is no darkness at all" (1 John 1:5). And the line is clear: "He who has the Son has the life. He who doesn't have God's Son doesn't have the life" (1 John 5:12).

Expect this reply

  • "These are symbols and lessons, not rules or history." Many Hindus read the stories this way, and say that being good is more important than the details of a story.

Your strongest answer

Then ask, gently: how does a person know which parts to take and which parts to leave? Where does the standard of "good" come from? The Bible answers that the standard is not a story but a person. God Himself is good, and He showed it fully in Jesus, who never fell short.

Four questions a Christian asks of the Vedas

The Vedas and Upanishads raise questions that follow the rest of this guide. Here are four, in plain words. Tap each to open it.

1. Who vouches for the Vedas?

What the tradition says. The Vedas are apaurusheya: no person made them. They are eternal sounds that ancient seers "heard", and that is why they have the highest authority.

The question. This claim comes from inside the Vedic tradition itself. Using the claim to prove the claim is circular reasoning (called petitio principii). Without an author, a date or a witness, there is nothing outside the tradition to check it against.

2. What happens to love and right and wrong?

What the tradition says. The Upanishads teach that your true self (Atman) is the same as the impersonal Brahman, in the line "Tat tvam asi", "That you are".

The question. If ultimate reality has no personality, love needs a lover and a beloved, so where does love come from? And personal responsibility and a relationship with God become part of Maya, the veil of appearances. The Trinity answers this: love exists between the Father, the Son and the Spirit before anything is made. This is the argument in "Monism and the personal God".

3. Is it tied to real events?

What the tradition says. Vedic authority rests on timeless truths about reality, not on events in history.

The question. Timeless ideas cannot be checked. The Bible is tied to testable events: God acting in the Exodus, His covenant with David, and the life, death and resurrection of Jesus in the first century. It stakes its claim on events that can be examined.

4. Where does karma begin?

What the tradition says. The cycle of births has no beginning, called anadi, and each life is shaped by the one before.

The question. A chain of causes that never began explains nothing about why there is suffering at all. This is the argument in "Karma and the endless chain". The Bible begins with a starting point, a good creation, and ends with a Judge who sets all things right.

Expect this reply

  • "Every way of knowing starts somewhere." Hindu thinkers say that tradition itself is a valid source of knowledge, like sight or logic, and that the Vedas are tested in the experience of those who practise them.

Your strongest answer

Agree that every view has starting points. The difference is whether those starting points can be checked from outside. A claim with no author, no date and no event behind it is hard to check. The Bible's claim names its witnesses and rests on one public event, and Jesus invites examination.

Keep it fair

Hindu belief is not one system, and this step is a short map, not the whole country. Different traditions stress different texts and different gods. Ask your friend which texts and which god matter most to them, and listen first.

Christ above all

Among all the ways people have pictured God, one is different. God did not stay in a story or a symbol: He became a person, "full of grace and truth" (John 1:14), and is good all the way through.
