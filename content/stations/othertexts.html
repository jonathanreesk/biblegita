The Hindu texts in depth

Law books, philosophy and devotional poems

Beyond the Vedas, the epics and the Puranas there is a large body of later writing. It shapes how Hindus live, think and worship every day.

Do they fit inside the Mahabharata?

No. All of these are separate and later. The Mahabharata has its own teaching on law and duty in the Shanti and Anushasana Parvas, but the law books, the philosophy texts, the temple manuals and the poems each stand on their own. Think of the Vedas and Upanishads as the roots, the epics and Puranas as the trunk, and these as the branches.

Law books (Dharma Shastras)

These set out duty (dharma) for each person: rules for family, society, rulers, rites and punishments. The best known is the Manusmriti, from about 200 BCE to 200 CE. It also sets out the four classes (varna) that later hardened into the caste system. Many Hindus today, including leaders of reform movements, do not accept all of it, and it is one of the most debated Hindu texts.

Theme: an ordered society where each person does their own duty. The problem: disorder. The way out: keep dharma. The goal: a good life now and a good rebirth.

Philosophy texts (the six schools)

The Upanishads left questions that the thinkers organized into six schools (darshanas). Their main texts are short "threads" (sutras) which need a commentary to be understood.

  • Vedanta and its Brahma Sutras. This is the school that reads the Upanishads, the Gita and the Brahma Sutras together. Its three famous teachers disagreed: Shankara taught that all is one (advaita), Ramanuja that God is personal and we are His body (qualified oneness), and Madhva that God and souls are always distinct (dvaita).
  • Yoga and the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali, about disciplining the mind to reach freedom.
  • Samkhya, which divides all reality into spirit and matter.
  • Nyaya, Vaisheshika and Mimamsa, about logic, the make-up of the world and the right reading of the Vedic rites.

The Christian question here is the same as in the earlier steps. If the highest teaching is that all is one, where is the place for love between a person and God? Ramanuja and Madhva saw that problem too, and many Hindus follow them.

Temple and ritual texts (Agamas and Tantras)

The Agamas are manuals for building temples, making images and running daily worship. Tantras are a related family of texts on ritual and the worship of the goddess. Most Hindu temple worship today follows an Agama. They are less read as scripture than followed as practice.

Devotional poems

From about 600 CE, poet-saints began writing songs of love for God in everyday languages instead of Sanskrit. They are the most widely sung Hindu texts.

  • The Alvars and Nayanars in Tamil, about Vishnu and Shiva.
  • Tulsidas, whose Ramcharitmanas retells the Ramayana in Hindi.
  • Jnaneshwar and Tukaram in Marathi, with Jnaneshwar's famous commentary on the Gita.
  • Kabir, Mirabai and Surdas, who sang of a God who is near and who loves.

Theme: God is near, and loves those who love Him. These songs are often what ordinary Hindus know best, far more than the Vedas.

Expect this reply

  • "All of this is one tradition, and each text speaks to a different person." That is how many Hindus see it. There is room for the thinker, the worker and the lover of God.

Your strongest answer

Say it kindly: it is a rich tradition, and the poems show how much people long for God. Then ask which of these voices is true when they disagree, for example over whether God and the soul are one or two. The Bible answers by pointing to a person, not an idea: Jesus, who said "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6).

Christ above all

The poets sang of a God who is near and who loves. Jesus is that nearness: He called His followers friends (John 15:15) and promised to be with them always (Matthew 28:20).
