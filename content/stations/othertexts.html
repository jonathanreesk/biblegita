Many texts

Law books, philosophy and devotional poems

Beyond the Vedas, the epics and the Puranas there is a large body of later Hindu writing. It shapes how Hindus live, think and worship every day. Each part has its own answer, and each can be put to the same fair test.

Law books (Dharma Shastras)

These set out duty (dharma) for each person: rules for family, society, rulers, rites and punishments. The best known is the Manusmriti, from about 200 BCE to 200 CE. It also sets out the four classes (varna) that later hardened into the caste system. Many Hindus today, including leaders of reform movements, do not accept all of it, and it is one of the most debated Hindu texts.

Philosophy texts (the six schools)

The Upanishads left questions that the thinkers organized into six schools (darshanas). Their main texts are short "threads" (sutras) which need a commentary to be understood.

  • Vedanta and its Brahma Sutras. This is the school that reads the Upanishads, the Bhagavad Gita and the Brahma Sutras together. Its three main teachers disagreed: Shankara taught that all is one (advaita), Ramanuja that God is personal and we are His body (qualified oneness), and Madhva that God and souls are always distinct (dvaita).
  • Yoga and the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali, about disciplining the mind to reach freedom.
  • Samkhya, which divides all reality into spirit and matter.
  • Nyaya, Vaisheshika and Mimamsa, about logic, the make-up of the world and the right reading of the Vedic rites.

Temple and ritual texts (Agamas and Tantras)

The Agamas are manuals for building temples, making images and running daily worship. Tantras are a related family of texts on ritual and the worship of the goddess. Most Hindu temple worship today follows an Agama. They are less read as scripture than followed as practice.

Devotional poems

From about 600 CE, poet-saints began writing songs of love for God in everyday languages instead of Sanskrit. They are the most widely sung Hindu texts.

  • The Alvars and Nayanars in Tamil, about Vishnu and Shiva.
  • Tulsidas, whose Ramcharitmanas retells the Ramayana in Hindi.
  • Jnaneshwar and Tukaram in Marathi, with Jnaneshwar's commentary on the Gita.
  • Kabir, Mirabai and Surdas, who sang of a God who is near and who loves.

Expect this reply

  • "All of this is one tradition, and each text speaks to a different person." There is room for the thinker, the worker and the lover of God.

Your strongest answer

When the voices disagree, which one is true? A tradition that holds opposite answers to who God is cannot tell you which one to trust. The Bible points to a person, not a school: Jesus, who said "I am the way, and the truth, and the life" (John 14:6).

What they ask, and what the Bible announces

These texts asks

How should I live, think and worship? Each answers with something to do: keep your duty, train your mind, serve the image, sing your love.

The Bible announces

You cannot fix yourself, but God makes you new: "If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation" (2 Corinthians 5:17).

Christ above all

The poets sang of a God who is near and who loves. Jesus is not a song about that God. He is that God, come near: He called His followers friends (John 15:15) and promised to be with them always (Matthew 28:20).
