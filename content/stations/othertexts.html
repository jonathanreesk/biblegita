The two stories

Law books, philosophy and devotional poems

Beyond the Vedas, the epics and the Puranas there is a large body of later Hindu writing. It shapes how Hindus live, think and worship every day. Each part has its own answer, and each one goes wrong somewhere.

Law books (Dharma Shastras)

These set out duty (dharma) for each person: rules for family, society, rulers, rites and punishments. The best known is the Manusmriti, from about 200 BCE to 200 CE. It also sets out the four classes (varna) that later hardened into the caste system. Many Hindus today, including leaders of reform movements, do not accept all of it, and it is one of the most debated Hindu texts.

Where it goes wrong

The Manusmriti ranks people by birth. It says the one duty given to the Shudra is to serve the three higher classes (Manusmriti 1.91), and that a woman "must never be independent" (5.148). Its punishments are harsher for lower classes. Many Hindus reject these parts today, but that means choosing which parts of the text to obey.

What the Bible says

Every person is made in God's image, "male and female" (Genesis 1:27). In Christ "there is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free man, there is neither male nor female" (Galatians 3:28).

Philosophy texts (the six schools)

The Upanishads left questions that the thinkers organized into six schools (darshanas). Their main texts are short "threads" (sutras) which need a commentary to be understood.

  • Vedanta and its Brahma Sutras. This is the school that reads the Upanishads, the Gita and the Brahma Sutras together. Its three main teachers disagreed: Shankara taught that all is one (advaita), Ramanuja that God is personal and we are His body (qualified oneness), and Madhva that God and souls are always distinct (dvaita).
  • Yoga and the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali, about disciplining the mind to reach freedom.
  • Samkhya, which divides all reality into spirit and matter.
  • Nyaya, Vaisheshika and Mimamsa, about logic, the make-up of the world and the right reading of the Vedic rites.

Where it goes wrong

The three Vedanta teachers read the same texts, the Upanishads, the Gita and the Brahma Sutras, and reached opposite answers to the most basic question: are God and the soul one, or two? If the texts were clear, they could not all claim them.

What the Bible says

The heart of the Bible is plain enough for a child: "Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day" (1 Corinthians 15:3-4).

Temple and ritual texts (Agamas and Tantras)

The Agamas are manuals for building temples, making images and running daily worship. Tantras are a related family of texts on ritual and the worship of the goddess. Most Hindu temple worship today follows an Agama. They are less read as scripture than followed as practice.

Where it goes wrong

The Agamas teach how to make an image and invite a god to live in it. But a god who must be housed, washed and fed by people is a god who depends on us.

What the Bible says

"The God who made the world and all things in it ... doesn't dwell in temples made with hands. He isn't served by men's hands, as though he needed anything" (Acts 17:24-25).

Devotional poems

From about 600 CE, poet-saints began writing songs of love for God in everyday languages instead of Sanskrit. They are the most widely sung Hindu texts.

  • The Alvars and Nayanars in Tamil, about Vishnu and Shiva.
  • Tulsidas, whose Ramcharitmanas retells the Ramayana in Hindi.
  • Jnaneshwar and Tukaram in Marathi, with Jnaneshwar's commentary on the Gita.
  • Kabir, Mirabai and Surdas, who sang of a God who is near and who loves.

Where it goes wrong

The poems express a longing for God. But the poets disagree: Kabir mocked idols and pilgrimages, while Tulsidas worshipped Rama as God. Longing for God is not the same as knowing Him, and no poem says how a sinner can be made right with Him.

What the Bible says

Jesus answers the longing with an invitation: "Come to me, all you who labor and are heavily burdened, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28).

Expect this reply

  • "All of this is one tradition, and each text speaks to a different person." There is room for the thinker, the worker and the lover of God.

Your strongest answer

When the voices disagree, which one is true? A tradition that holds opposite answers to who God is cannot tell you which one to trust. The Bible points to a person, not a school: Jesus, who said "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6).

What they ask, and what the Bible announces

These texts asks

How should I live, think and worship? Each answers with something to do: keep your duty, train your mind, serve the image, sing your love.

The Bible announces

You cannot fix yourself, but God makes you new: "If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation" (2 Corinthians 5:17).

Christ above all

The poets sang of a God who is near and who loves. Jesus is not a song about that God. He is that God, come near: He called His followers friends (John 15:15) and promised to be with them always (Matthew 28:20).
