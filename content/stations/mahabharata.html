The two stories

The Mahabharata and the Gita

The Gita is not a book on its own. It is one conversation inside the Mahabharata, the longest Hindu epic, about a family at war. Before comparing ideas, know the plot: who is who, what happens, and whether anyone in it is truly good.

The Mahabharata: 18 parvas about a family war

Tradition credits the sage Vyasa with the Mahabharata, a poem of about 100,000 verses divided into 18 sections, each called a Parva in Sanskrit. It grew over several centuries and was told and retold by many people, mostly through spoken performance, so versions differ from region to region. What follows is the widely shared core of the story. Its central question is what dharma (duty) demands when duties collide.

Who is who: two sides of one family

Both sides are cousins from the same royal family. This is not one people fighting outsiders. It is a family tearing itself apart, and nearly every name below is related to another.

The Pandavas

The five sons of the late king Pandu.

  • Yudhishthira The eldest, known for truthfulness. He gambles the kingdom away.
  • Bhima The second, the strongest.
  • Arjuna The third, the finest archer. He is the one who speaks with Krishna in the Gita.
  • Nakula and Sahadeva The twins, the youngest two.
  • Draupadi Their wife, shamed in open court.
  • Kunti Their mother.
The Kauravas

The hundred sons of the blind king Dhritarashtra, who hold the throne.

  • Duryodhana The eldest, who refuses to share the kingdom.
  • Dushasana His brother, who drags Draupadi into the court.
  • Dhritarashtra Their blind father, the king.
  • Gandhari Their mother, who lost all her sons and curses Krishna.
  • Shakuni Their uncle, who tricks Yudhishthira at dice.
Krishna: neither side

Krishna is not a Pandava or a Kaurava. He is the Pandavas' cousin and Arjuna's close friend. Before the war he offered his great army to one side and himself, without weapons, to the other. Duryodhana chose the army. Arjuna chose Krishna. So Krishna fights for no one: he drives Arjuna's chariot and speaks the Gita. Many Hindus worship him as God.

Fighting for the Kauravas, out of loyalty
  • Bhishma The grand elder of the family, bound by a vow to defend the throne.
  • Drona The teacher who trained both the Pandavas and the Kauravas.
  • Karna A great archer who is secretly the Pandavas' eldest brother, born to Kunti before her marriage. He fights for Duryodhana, his only friend.
The tellers of the story
  • Vyasa The poet credited with the Mahabharata. He is also the grandfather of both families.
  • Sanjaya The king's minister, who tells Dhritarashtra what happens on the battlefield, including the Gita.

Four moments of the war worth knowing

The eighteen days of war are remembered for four moments when the side said to stand for dharma won by breaking a rule. Tap each to see what happened and why it matters. Details differ a little between regional versions of the epic.

Drona and "Ashwatthama is dead"

What happened. The teacher Drona was the Kauravas' commander and could not be beaten while he held his weapons. Bhima killed an elephant named Ashwatthama, the same name as Drona's beloved son, and shouted that Ashwatthama was dead. Drona would believe only Yudhishthira, who never lied. Yudhishthira said "Ashwatthama is dead," and added quietly "man or elephant". Krishna had the war conches blown so that Drona did not hear the last words. Drona put down his weapons in grief and was killed.

Why it matters. Even the most truthful man gave a half-truth to win. The epic says that until then Yudhishthira's chariot moved a little above the ground, a sign of his truthfulness, and from that day it touched the earth. Victory cost him his purity.

Karna and the stuck chariot

What happened. In his last duel with Arjuna, Karna's chariot wheel sank into the mud. He climbed down to free it and asked Arjuna to wait, as the rules of war say a man in trouble should not be struck. Krishna answered that Karna had not kept those rules when Draupadi was shamed or when the young Abhimanyu was killed by many warriors at once. Arjuna shot, and Karna fell.

Why it matters. It raises a hard question: can you claim the protection of the rules after you broke them yourself? Readers have argued about it ever since, and it is one of the moments when Krishna's guidance looks most like "the end justifies the means".

Bhishma and the shield of Shikhandi

What happened. The grand elder Bhishma could choose the hour of his own death and could not be beaten in battle. He had vowed never to fight a woman. Shikhandi had been born a woman, so Bhishma would not raise his weapons against Shikhandi. Arjuna stood behind Shikhandi and shot arrows past, and Bhishma fell onto a bed of arrows.

Why it matters. The Pandavas won by using a loophole in the honor code of a man they loved. Bhishma himself had told them how to defeat him, which shows that this was no simple wrong. It shows that even the noblest defeat in the epic is morally mixed.

Duryodhana struck below the belt

What happened. In a duel with maces, the rule was never to strike below the waist. After a long fight, Krishna signaled to Bhima by slapping his own thigh. Bhima remembered his vow to break Duryodhana's thigh for what had been done to Draupadi, struck there, and Duryodhana fell. Krishna's brother Balarama, who had taught Duryodhana, was furious at the foul blow.

Why it matters. Here the rule of fair fighting collides with a promise of revenge. The Pandavas choose the vow over the rule, and the epic lets even Krishna's own brother call it wrong.

Taken together, the epic is telling us that this war had no clean victory. The side said to be right could win only by doing wrong, with Krishna's guidance, and the cost follows them to the end. Gandhari, who lost all her sons, curses Krishna: his own clan will destroy itself, and it does. This is why many Hindus say the Mahabharata teaches that dharma is "subtle": hard to see, and costly even when you see it.

How the Gita fits: the whole epic is being told to the blind king Dhritarashtra by his minister Sanjaya, which is why the Gita begins with the king asking what his sons did on the battlefield. Scholars debate when the Gita was placed into the Mahabharata, but within the story it is one conversation, held just before the fighting begins.

Where it goes wrong: so who is the good one?

A reader will ask who the heroes and villains are. The two books answer very differently.

Is there a clear good side?
The Mahabharata

Not clearlyTradition sees the Pandavas as standing for dharma and Duryodhana for its opposite. But the epic itself will not make it simple. Both sides break the rules of war, and the Pandavas win partly by deceit, as the four moments above show. Honorable men fight for the Kauravas out of loyalty.

The Bible

Yes, and it is not usThe Bible names one who is good: God. "No one is good except one—God" (Mark 10:18). Everyone else, on every side, has fallen short, with one exception: Jesus.

Where do the heroes fall short?
The Mahabharata

Everywhere, and the epic lets it standYudhishthira, the truthful one, gambles away his wife. The victors rule over ruins, and Gandhari curses even Krishna. The epic often says that dharma is "subtle", meaning hard to see.

The Bible

Everywhere, except oneThe Bible tells the failures of its own heroes: Abraham, Moses, David and Peter. "There is no one righteous; no, not one" (Romans 3:10). "All have sinned, and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23). With one exception, below.

Is there anyone who never falls short?
The Mahabharata

No oneEvery character fails somewhere, and even Krishna's part in the war's deceits is questioned inside the epic.

The Bible

One: Jesus, the perfect man and the perfect GodHe is fully human and fully God (John 1:14, Colossians 2:9). He was tempted in every way as we are, yet without sin (Hebrews 4:15). "He committed no sin, neither was deceit found in his mouth" (1 Peter 2:22). Jesus never fell short, not once.

Who is the enemy?
The Mahabharata

Other peopleThe enemy is a rival family, and in the end the cost falls on everyone.

The Bible

Sin and an evil spirit, and the heartThe deceiver, Satan, is the enemy behind it (Revelation 12:9), but the problem also lies within every person. No human side is the good side. The only good one is Jesus, God made man.

Expect this reply

  • "That is the point of the Mahabharata." Many Hindus say the epic is deliberately honest that life is morally mixed, with no one fully right, and that wisdom comes from thinking hard about that.

Your strongest answer

A story that leaves good and evil unsettled cannot tell you which side you are on, or what to do about the wrong in your own heart. The Bible gives a clear standard, God Himself, and a clear verdict: no one meets it, except one. Jesus never fell short, and that is exactly why He can save us. He is the answer that a confused and broken world needs. Christ, the one who is righteous, died "the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God" (1 Peter 3:18).

What it asks, and what the Bible announces

The Mahabharata asks

What is the right thing to do when every choice costs something? It never settles the answer, and the story ends in grief.

The Bible announces

What has God done about a broken world? Its answer is a rescue, and the story ends in restoration.

Christ above all

The Mahabharata ends in grief: the victors have lost nearly everyone they love, and no one in the story is truly good. The Bible ends with Jesus Christ, the Lamb who was slain, honored by all creation (Revelation 5:12). He is the one who never fell short.
