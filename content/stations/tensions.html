Can the texts be trusted?

Hard passages in both texts

Every long religious text has passages that readers find hard to fit together. A fair conversation names the difficulties in your own text first, then asks how your friend handles theirs. The Hindu questions below are ones Hindu teachers and readers discuss among themselves. The Christian ones are the objections you will hear.

Hard questions Hindu readers ask

War and non-violence

Krishna urges Arjuna to fight, telling him there is "nothing higher for a Kshatriya than a righteous war" (Gita 2:31) and warning of sin if he refuses (Gita 2:33). Yet ahimsa, non-harm, is among Hinduism's highest values. Some read the battle as an inner struggle. Others take it literally.

Which path saves?

The Gita names a path of knowledge and a path of action (Gita 3:3), and teaches devotion elsewhere. Great commentators disagree over which one the Gita finally puts first. Shankara stressed knowledge, and Ramanuja stressed devotion.

Keep your duty, or leave all duties?

"Better is one's own duty, though devoid of merit, than the duty of another" (Gita 3:35). Yet the Gita ends: "Abandon all duties and take refuge in Me alone" (Gita 18:66). Readers debate how the two fit together.

The Vedas honored and criticized

Krishna criticizes those who take pleasure in the "flowery speech" of the Vedas (Gita 2:42), then calls himself the one "to be known by all the Vedas" (Gita 15:15).

The Mahabharata raises a deeper problem of its own: the side said to stand for dharma wins by breaking the rules of war, as the four moments in "The Mahabharata and the Gita" showed.

Where the Hindu texts differ from one another

The Hindu texts were composed over about two thousand years by different schools. They sometimes give different answers to the same question. Hindu teachers know this, and most explain it as layers or as different paths for different people. Here are the clearest examples, each with the usual Hindu reply.

Who is the Supreme?

The early Rigveda praises Indra, Agni and Varuna, and gives Vishnu and Rudra (later Shiva) little space. The Vaishnava Puranas make Vishnu supreme, with Brahma born from a lotus in his navel (Bhagavata Purana 3.8). The Shaiva Puranas tell of Vishnu and Brahma failing to find the end of Shiva's pillar of light (Shiva Purana). The Shakta texts place the Goddess above all three. The Upanishads speak of Brahman beyond any form. Common reply: these are different faces of one reality, or different devotions for different people.

How did creation happen?

Rigveda 10.129 asks whether even the highest one knows how creation began. Rigveda 10.121 speaks of a golden womb or egg. Rigveda 10.90 tells of a cosmic person offered in sacrifice. The Upanishads speak of the world coming out of Brahman. Common reply: these are poetic attempts at a mystery, not rival reports, and the question itself is left open.

Personal God or impersonal Brahman?

Some Upanishads describe Brahman without qualities (nirguna). The Gita and the Bhagavata Purana describe a personal God who loves and who is worshipped. Common reply: the personal form is a way to reach the formless, which is Shankara's reading. Ramanuja and Madhva read the other way, with God personal at the very top.

Meat and non-harm

The Vedas describe animal sacrifice, and the epics mention hunting and eating game, though scholars debate some of the Ramayana verses. Later texts, such as the Puranas and parts of the Manusmriti, strongly praise abstaining from meat and say ahimsa is the highest duty. Even the Manusmriti permits meat in some circumstances while praising those who refrain. Common reply: ideals changed as society changed, and the later ideal is the higher one.

Images of God

Yajurveda 40.9, which is also verse 12 of the Isha Upanishad, says that those who worship the unmanifest enter "blind darkness", and those who delight in the manifest enter greater darkness. Some Hindus read it as a warning against worshipping created things and images. Others do not. The Puranas and the Agamas, meanwhile, give detailed rules for temples, images and the rite that invites the deity into the image. Common reply: the image is a help for the worshipper and not the limit of God.

Rama and Krishna

In the Ramayana, Rama keeps his word whatever it costs. In the Mahabharata, Krishna advises the Pandavas to break the war's rules in the deaths of Drona, Karna and Duryodhana. Common reply: they came in different ages, and dharma is "subtle". The Mahabharata lets its own characters object.

Expect this reply

  • "These are not contradictions, only layers and paths." Many Hindus hold this. But layers that disagree about who God is cannot all be true about God.

Your strongest answer

Ask gently: if the texts disagree about who God is, how does a person decide which to trust? Hindus have an answer: the Shruti texts rank above the Smriti texts, and a teacher guides the reader. A Christian can then say honestly that the Bible makes one claim that stays the same from Genesis to Revelation: one God, who is personal, and who finally reveals Himself in Jesus (John 1:14). Then invite them to look at the evidence for that claim.

Notice the difference. The Bible's hard passages, below, are details around one event, and each has a reasonable answer. The Hindu texts disagree about who God is. The God of the Bible says, "I, Yahweh, don't change" (Malachi 3:6), and He does not contradict Himself. Say this gently, but say it.

Hard questions Christian readers face

Two family trees for Jesus

Matthew 1 and Luke 3 list different ancestors between David and Jesus. Common reply: Matthew follows the legal line through Joseph, the royal heir, and Luke most likely follows Mary's family line. Ancient genealogies had different purposes.

Two accounts of Judas's death

Matthew says Judas hanged himself (Matthew 27:5). Acts says he fell headlong and burst open (Acts 1:18). Common reply: the accounts can describe two stages of one event, with the hanging first and the fall after. Not every reader finds this tidy.

Resurrection details differ

The four Gospels differ on how many women went to the tomb and how many angels they met. Common reply: each writer selects from a larger event. All agree that the tomb was empty, that women were the first witnesses, and that Jesus was alive. Paul lists the witnesses in 1 Corinthians 15:3-8.

Faith or works?

Paul: "a man is justified by faith apart from the works of the law" (Romans 3:28). James: "by works, a man is justified, and not only by faith" (James 2:24). Common reply: they use "justified" and "works" for different things, and both say that real faith produces good works (Ephesians 2:8-10).

Command to destroy, command to love

God commands Israel to destroy certain Canaanite cities (Deuteronomy 20:16-17), while Jesus says "love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44). Common reply: the first was a one-time judgment on particular nations at a particular time, never a standing order, and Jesus fulfills the Old Testament and does not repeat that command. Christians still find it hard, and you can say so.

Expect this reply

  • "Those are proof of human editing." Notice that the differences are about details. Independent witnesses rarely match word for word, and identical accounts would suggest they were coordinated.
  • "Your answers are guesses." Some are. Say so honestly. Then ask what a Hindu reader does with the questions above. Honest readers of both texts keep reading.

Your strongest answer

A text can have hard passages and still be true at its center. The question is what each text stakes everything on. The Bible stakes everything on one public event: "If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain, and your faith also is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14). The difficulties above are about details around that event, not about whether Jesus died and rose. Invite your friend to weigh the evidence for the event itself.

Gentleness is the point. You are not scoring points against a text your friend loves. You are showing that you have read yours with the same honesty you ask of theirs.

With the reliability of the texts and the hard questions now laid out, the next step follows the one thread that runs through the whole Bible, and where it leads.

Christ above all

Hard passages do not move the center. Christ is the key to all Scripture (Luke 24:27), and He does not change: Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, today and forever (Hebrews 13:8).
